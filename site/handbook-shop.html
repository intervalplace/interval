◆

This book describes world 1c14358e162a. That is not a version number: it is a checksum of the rules. Change one character of them and you get a different world with a different name. Nothing in this book can go out of date, because nothing in the rules can change.

2

About interval

Interval is an online world run by the computers of the people playing it. There is no company behind it and no server that owns it. There is no account either. You have a key, and the key is your citizen.

The rules were set when the world was founded and cannot be changed by anyone, including the people who wrote them. That is why nobody can ban you, patch you or shut it down, and why this book can be printed at all.

This book lists the rules: the crafts, the items, the recipes, the spells and the levels. It does not tell you what to do, where anything is, or how to make money. Those are not rules, and you work them out by playing.

◆

How the world is drawn is not part of the rules. The window you are using is one way of showing it and there is no official one. You can write your own and it counts the same.

3

The interval

The world advances one interval every second, whether or not anyone is playing. Everything happens on an interval: a step, an attack, a log cut, a line of chat.

Nothing speeds this up. There is nothing to buy, and the world does not pause when you log off.

◆

Ninety minutes

One citizen may stand in the world for 90 minutes in any 24 hours. It is in the rules, so every window enforces it and no window can be talked out of it.

It is not a day. There is no midnight and no reset: that window rolls, so the time you spent comes back gradually as it falls behind you rather than all at once at an hour somebody else picked. There is nothing to use up before it expires and nothing to race for.

Only what you DO spends it. The world counts you present if you have acted within the last minute, or if something you set going is still running, and it charges you for that whole minute. Standing about costs nothing once a minute has gone by, and closing the window costs nothing at all.

You are told 10 minutes before it closes.

4

Closing time

When it is spent you are not thrown out and nothing is taken from you. No craft falls, no vault empties, no ground is lost. Your citizen stands where they stood, holding what they held.

What you cannot do is act. Every deed is refused at the one gate they all pass through, so there is nothing to look for and nothing that slips past it.

You can still talk, near and across the island, and you can still be talked to. The end of a day's work is not the end of the evening.

As the window rolls, the time comes back and you may act again. Nobody has to let you back in.

A second pair of keys is not a way around this. It is the price of it: new keys are a new citizen, with no crafts, no standing, no calling, no name and no vault.

6

Swearing to a trade

You can swear to one trade once you have done two things: reached 50 in its craft, and stood in 5 of the island's 7 countries. It is the only craft you can ever master.

The second half is there because the first comes quickly. Reaching 50 in a craft takes under two hours of work and can be done standing at one rock, and this is the only choice in a citizen's life that cannot be taken back. So the world asks you to have seen something of it first. It keeps the count itself as you walk; you do not have to tell it anything.

Neither the Wilds nor the Moor is needed. 5 is the peaceful island, all of it, so nobody has to be hunted to take up a trade.

Unswornnothing passes 50
Outside your tradenothing passes 70
Your own craftmastery at 100

Nothing stacks up across crafts. Each citizen masters one craft and stays middling at the other eight, so most of what you need you will get from other players.

7

Being taken on

Swearing costs nothing and happens wherever you are. It takes no interval, interrupts nothing, and needs no altar and no official: saying what you are is not an action, and a citizen halfway through a journey does not stop to say it.

When you swear you may name the master who taught you. They have to be standing with you, they have to be a master of the same craft at 100, and they have to have taken you on and not let it lapse: an apprenticeship is forgotten after 12 hours.

Naming one is optional, and swearing alone is a real swearing. The first woodwright on the island had nobody to name, and anyone playing at a quiet hour would otherwise be stuck waiting for a master to wake up. The mark is a reward; its absence is not a wall.

◆

What the mark does is make masters. Reaching 100 in your own craft only makes you eligible; you become a master when somebody you took on swears, and names you. That cannot be done alone and cannot be ground out.

Swearing also ends every apprenticeship you were in, whoever took you on and whatever trade it was to. You may be taken on by a forester, walk off and swear miner: you promised nothing, and your master consented to teach rather than to be owed.

8

What you are called

Until you swear you are a newcomer, however good you are at anything. The world does not name you after your best craft, because before you swear you are not of that trade: that is the whole of what swearing is for.

If a master has taken you on you are an apprentice instead, which is worth knowing about a stranger: somebody is teaching them, and you can ask who.

Once you swear, the word becomes your calling and never changes again. A smith who spends a year at the trees is still a smith.

newcomerUnsworn, and nobody has taken them on.
apprenticeUnsworn, but a master has taken them on.
journeymanSworn to a trade.
masterAt 100, and has raised somebody.
9

Past mastery

master goes in front of your word once you have mastered the craft and raised somebody to their own swearing: the number alone does not buy it.

There is no title for mastering everything, because there is no mastering everything. One craft to a citizen is the whole of the rule, and the caps hold you to it.

Mastery is a power cap, not a level cap. Everything in the world uses your level or 100, whichever is lower, so nothing you do gets stronger past it. Levels carry on to 210 and count for nothing but showing how long you have played.

They do give you apprentice slots, one at each of 100, 105, 110, so a high-level master can teach more people.

10

Teaching

Reaching 100 in your own craft does not make you a master. It makes you eligible. You become a master by raising somebody else to their own swearing, and until you have, you are not one.

It cannot be done alone and it cannot be ground out: it needs another citizen to reach 50 and swear, and they have to agree to be taken on in the first place.

A master holds 3 places at most, and an apprentice who swears frees their own place. One who simply stops coming back releases it after about 12 hours.

Whoever raised you is recorded against your swearing and stays there. It cannot be traded, lost or undone.

◆

A master takes 2 where anyone else takes one, and where there is nothing to double, their weapon comes back a quarter sooner.

11

The things there is only one of

Everything in the pages that follow is interchangeable: one log is any log. Two things are not. Each carries whose it was and the interval it was made on, and no two are the same object.

a wayfarer's hood Given for walking every trade. It has no defence and no price, and it survives the death that takes everything else.
a fall stone The last rock off a boulder somebody finished. The same rock as any rubble; the difference is who broke it and when.
◆

None of them can be bought from a keeper, because a keeper deals in kinds and these have no kind. They change hands between people or not at all.

What one is worth is decided years after it is made, by whose name turns out to be on it. That cannot be known in advance and cannot be run ahead of.

12

The fires

Bars are run at a furnace and the furnace has to be burning. A cold one is a pile of stone and will smelt nothing, however much ore you are carrying.

Coal or charcoal feeds it, one piece at a time. A coal is worth 540 intervals of heat and a charcoal 360, and it will not hold more than 3600 intervals however much you put in. A fire cannot be filled once and left for a week.

Anyone may feed anyone's fire. You earn 26 earthcraft for the piece you put in, at your own fire or a stranger's, and whoever fed it last earns a little every interval it goes on burning. Feeding a full fire still spends the fuel and still pays you.

The watchfire is the other public fire. It takes logs or coal, and ironbark and coal burn three times as long as an ordinary log.

13

Who works here

A work remembers the last 5 people who used it and how long ago, and tells you when you stand beside it. The anvil, the furnace, the sawpit and the mill keep this, and so does each fire, for whoever fed it or burned it down for charcoal.

It forgets a name after 6000 intervals.

This is the only thing in the world that will tell you who else does what you do. It says who works here; it does not say where they are, and there is no list anywhere of who is about. You still have to walk to the furnace to find out who works the furnace, and then go and find them.

15

Keeping

Your pack holds 12 things and that is all you can carry. Everything else lives in a vault, and a vault belongs to the counter it stands at: what you put away in one town is in that town, not waiting for you in the next one.

You may hold up to 32 of them, one per counter. Crafting only ever reads your pack, never a vault, so what you take out before you leave is what you have.

◆

Hauling

A store buys nothing and sells nothing. What it does is sign a consignment: goods entered at one counter, carried by you, and given up at another. The route may run to 8 stops.

It pays in wayfaring, not in coin, and it pays by the tile and by how much of the load you are carrying. A long road with a full pack is the whole of the wage.

The consignment is not your pack. It is a second container a vault cannot reach and you cannot eat out of.

16

Your own stall

A keeper's counter is not the only place goods change hands. You may raise a stall of your own: 10 planks and 2 iron ore, and 20 intervals of standing still while you build it. One each.

It holds one kind of good, up to 200 of it, at whatever price you set. Anybody may buy from it while you are elsewhere, and the coin waits for you to come and take it. Left untouched it falls down.

The spells

There are 10 spells, split between two spellbooks. A citizen has one book or the other, never both. Casting a spell uses up a sigil.

The other spellbook. These are the ones that harm.

27

What a spell leaves on you

A spell is cast in one interval and is over. What it leaves on the person it hit is not, and there are six of these. You will meet them before you ever cast anything.

stilledYou cannot strike. It wears off.
rootedYou cannot move from where you stand.
burningYou lose health every interval until it goes out.
rottingThe same, slower, and it came from the barrow book.
witheredYour blows land for less while it lasts.
brandedA mark on you that others can see.
◆

Each of them ends by itself after a number of intervals. Nothing you carry removes one early, and dying does not clear them either.

You can see all six on somebody from across a square, which is the only warning this world gives you that a stranger has already cast.

28

Dying

When you are killed you drop everything you were carrying. It lies on the ground where you fell, for anyone to pick up, and it rots after a while like anything else left out.

The world pauses over you for 5 intervals and then gives you back whole, at Anchor, wherever on the island you fell. Nothing is taken from your crafts: levels do not fall here, and no trade, name or standing is touched.

It does leave a wound, which is permanent and does not heal with rest. One thing in the world clears them. See what dying costs.

Which is also the long way home. Dying in the Wilds costs you what you were carrying and the walk back out.

29

What is counted

Your deaths are counted, forever, and the number never falls. It is not a punishment and nothing in the world reads it except the boards. You are free to farm it if you want to, at the price of everything you are carrying each time.

Kills are not counted, and that is deliberate. A kill count would be worth something, and the cheapest way to earn it would be to make new citizens and cut them down. A death count is a joke you paid for.

◆

What can be saved

At mourning 70 the most valuable priced thing you are carrying survives your death. Priced means a stall somewhere asks a price for it: the ore, the blades, the plate. What this world is really worth keeping is on no such list, and a sigil, a chart or an old chain is exactly as losable as it always was.

Somebody with a goo-staff can also seal your dropped pack where it lies, which holds it for you and stops it rotting. See the spells.

30

The Wilds

Most of the island is ordinary ground, where nobody may strike you without answering for it. The Wilds are not. Anybody may hunt anybody there, and nobody is coming.

Everything worth the most is there, which is the trade: the quick rock, the mother lode, the deep water, the gallows-oaks. You cannot reach the end of any craft without going, and every trip back is carrying something worth taking.

Magic will not carry you out of it, and nothing else will: this world has no way to step out of one place and into another. You walk out of the Wilds or you die in them.

◆

The Lists

An isle off Fenmarch, reached by boat. Anybody may strike anybody, and no spell from either book works at all.

The first crossing needs a charter, spent on the way out. After that you are chartered for life, and the island is told.

A charter is drawn from a chart by a wayfarer at 90; a chart is what a surveyor of 90 brings home. The way onto the Lists runs through somebody else's mastery.

It is for a fight that is only about the fight: with the magic gone, a mell and a bare blade can be compared honestly.

31

The tide

The tide is the world's own clock, worked out from the interval count and nothing else. It is the same for everybody everywhere, nobody chose it, and nobody can move it.

It gates nothing and costs nothing. What it is for is that anyone can say when the next one falls, and be right.

the deep tide up for 30 minutes, every 12 hours
◆

It is announced when it turns, and the announcement says where people actually stood at the last one. Nobody chose that place either: it stays the place only while people keep going there.

It does not fall at the same hour twice. Two turns are twenty minutes longer than a day, so it slips twenty minutes later each day and works right round the clock in thirty-six. No part of the world is permanently asleep when it falls.

This is the closest thing the world has to an appointment. "At the deep tide" is something two strangers can agree on without either of them being able to move it.

Gambits

Seven weapons have a move of their own. Blows is how many it lands. Recovery is how many intervals your arm is spent for afterwards. Costs is that recovery measured against the weapon's ordinary swing: how many normal blows you gave up to make this one.

33

The five kinds

flurryseveral blows in one go, each rolled to hit as usual
wholeone blow at the weapon's hardest, and harder to land
nowdoes not wait for your arm after an ordinary swing
farthe damage comes from the range, not the weapon
reportone shot carrying all of it, and the least likely to land
◆

A far shot is weaker than a dagger at touching range and the hardest blow in the world at the end of its nine tiles. The range is the whole of its damage, not an addition to it.

A report is the handgonne alone. Its burst was scaled down when a citizen's health became a flat sixty-four, and all of the damage went into the single shot: the hardest blow in the world, and the only gambit more likely to miss than to land.

What a gambit costs is not the same for every weapon. The recovery is measured against that weapon's own swing, so the cost column on the previous page is how many ordinary blows you gave up for it: four for a quick dagger, two for a mell, less than one for a handgonne.

Each kind is meant to pay that cost back in its own coin. A flurry pays in blows, a whole in size, a now in timing, a far in distance.

Master your own craft and the arm comes back a quarter sooner, which is what prowess gives instead of hitting harder.

34

Eating

Food is a rate, not a burst. A meal does not jump your health up the moment you swallow it: it mends you steadily over the intervals that follow, and a richer meal simply mends for longer.

What the better food buys is the SIZE of one swallow. That is what gets a wounded citizen out of reach of a finisher in a single interval, and it is why the best food in the world is worth carrying even though everything heals at much the same speed.

And a meal spends the arm. Eating is not fighting: the interval you spend on it is an interval you did not swing in, which is the whole of what stops anybody eating their way through a duel.

47

What dying costs

You wake at the spawn. Everything in your pack is gone and anybody may take it from where you fell. Your trades, your name, your standing and your coin survive, which is why selling to a keeper is how a day's work stops being something you can lose.

And you take a wound. One permanent point off your frame, every time, to a limit of 10. Nothing you can carry and no amount of resting gives it back. An ordinary well restores your health to full and does not touch it.

One thing in the world clears them, and it clears all of them at once. It is a wellspring. There is no second way, it is not in any town, and this book will not say where it is: that is the one scarcity here that cannot be read out of the founding, and telling you would spend it. The world announces that somebody has come back whole, so you will know it has been found.

◆

A citizen at the limit has 10 points fewer than one who has never died, for as long as they go without finding it.

48

Things nobody sells

A price is what a stall ASKS for a thing. Nothing here has one: no stall sells it, some of it cannot be carried at all, and none of it can be worked out by trying.

torch Burns for 900 intervals from the moment it is lit, and burns them whether it is in your hand or in your pack. The Smother will not let you in without a light, and it asks only on the way in: you may always walk out and light another, and the walk is what it costs you.
forage Left by a goblin, a wolf or a bear about a third of the time they fall. It cannot be picked up, carried, banked, traded or priced. If this world stops

A world keeps its interval because its witnesses sign each one. If they all stop, the clock stops with them. Nothing about this can be worked out by playing, so it is written here.

Your citizen is not held by anybody else. A file is kept for you beside your key, every ten minutes, and it holds your whole record: your levels, your money, your pack, your name, what you swore, the countries you have walked and the people you know. In the browser it is kept in the browser, and there is a button to save a copy off it. The file cannot be edited. Add a level to it and it stops matching the mark the world signed, and then it is worth nothing.

◆

Anybody may found a world that continues this one. It must use the same rules, the same seed and the same island, and it names one mark: the one this world ended on. It does not name you, or anybody. That is what lets it be founded by somebody who holds nothing of yours.

You come back by bringing your file. Ask any node for your way in, which is a few hundred bytes it will give to anyone, and then say that you are coming home. The way in goes stale as soon as somebody else comes home, so ask for it when you need it rather than keeping it.

Your file is the part worth keeping.

50

Coming home

You do not have to do any of that by hand. When nothing answers for your world, the window asks every node it has ever spoken to where the world went, checks the answer against the founding your own file carries, and goes there. It will tell you before it moves you. If somebody hands you an address, use it: being told by a person is still better than taking the first answer, because the window can check that a world is the same world and cannot check that it is the one everybody else went to.

One file, one homecoming. You may come back once, and what you bring is what a crossing brings: your levels, your money, your goods, your name if nobody here has taken it, and every tally that only ever grew. Where you were standing does not cross, and neither does anything you left on the ground. You wake where newcomers wake.

◆

So a world can stop and the line of it cannot. There is nothing to shut down that would take your citizen with it.
